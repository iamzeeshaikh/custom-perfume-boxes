# Lead Times and Planning Backward

Packaging deadlines are missed at predictable points, and almost never during printing. This is what each stage actually takes and where the time really goes.

## The published windows

**Handling: 2 to 3 business days.** This begins when you approve the digital proof, not when you send artwork. It covers order verification, design finalization, plate preparation, printing, finishing and packing.

**Transit: 3 to 6 business days.** Ground freight from dispatch to your address. Shipping is free on all orders with no minimum order value.

Worst case, that is nine business days from proof approval to delivery. Best case it is five. Plan against nine and treat five as luck.

## The stage that is not in those windows

Everything above starts at proof approval. What comes before it is:

1. You send artwork and a specification
2. We prepare a digital proof
3. You check it and approve or request changes
4. Each change round repeats steps 2 and 3

Step 3 is where deadlines die. Production is a machine with a known throughput; approval is a person with other work, and a proof that sits in an inbox for four days has consumed most of a typical buffer before anything has been printed.

It is also the only stage entirely within your control. If a date matters, clear time for the proof before you send the artwork, not after.

## Working backward from a fixed date

For an immovable date — a trade show, a market week, a launch, a retailer's on-shelf date — count backward:

| Working backward from | Business days |
| --- | --- |
| Delivery to your address | 0 |
| Dispatch from production | 3–6 |
| Proof approval | 5–9 |
| Proof issued to you | 6–11 |
| Artwork received by us | 7–13 |

Then add your own approval time honestly. If your organization takes a week to sign anything off, three weeks ahead of the date is the realistic starting point, not ten days.

## What genuinely adds time

**A new structure.** A custom shape needs a dieline drawn, a physical cutting die manufactured and usually a hand-built mock-up approved. That sits in front of the normal handling window. Standard structures skip all of it because the die already exists.

**A printed sample.** Produced on your real stock with your real finish, so it takes a production slot of its own. It adds a few days and it is the only way to see final color before committing, which on color-critical work is worth every one of them.

**Peak season.** Gift packaging demand concentrates into a few weeks, and mills, laminators and finishers all hit capacity together. A job that takes eight business days in March can take three weeks in October, and the constraint is usually finishing and ribbon supply rather than press time.

**Wood and specialty materials.** [Engraved wooden boxes](/product/perfume-wooden-boxes/) are a joinery process rather than a printing one, and they run to a different, longer schedule.

## What does not add as much time as people expect

Extra finishes are mostly parallel rather than sequential in scheduling terms, so a second finish rarely doubles anything. Larger quantities barely move the calendar either: the setup dominates, and a 5,000-unit run is not five times the elapsed time of a 1,000-unit run.

If you are choosing between a simpler box delivered sooner and the box you actually want, the gap is usually smaller than it feels.

## Reorders are faster

Once a job has run, the plates and any custom die exist. A repeat order skips dieline work, skips tooling and usually skips the proof round entirely, which removes the least predictable stage from the schedule.

This is the practical argument for ordering the [100-unit minimum](/design-your-box/) first and reordering against real demand, rather than committing to a large run before you know the number.
