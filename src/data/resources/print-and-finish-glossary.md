# Print and Finish Glossary

Quotes for packaging are full of terms that sound interchangeable and are not. This is what each one means, what it does to the box, and where the combinations go wrong.

## Color

### CMYK, or process color

Four inks — cyan, magenta, yellow, black — overlaid in dots to build every other color. It handles photographs, gradients and multi-color artwork, and it is the economical choice whenever the design has more than three colors in it.

Its weakness is repeatability. Process color drifts slightly between runs, and while nobody notices that in isolation, it becomes obvious when this quarter's boxes sit beside last quarter's.

### Pantone, or spot color

A pre-mixed ink made to a published formula and printed from its own unit on the press. Because the ink itself is the color rather than an optical illusion built from dots, it lands identically on every run.

Use a spot ink when the color is a brand asset — when it also appears on your bottle label, your website and your reorders. The extra plate cost is recovered the first time a repeat order matches without a conversation about it.

### Rich black

Solid black printed as 100% K alone comes out flat and slightly grey. A rich black adds cyan beneath the black to give it depth. Any box with large black areas should be set up this way, which is how our [matte black cartons](/product/black-perfume-boxes/) are prepared.

### Colors that fall outside the printable range

Some screen colors have no CMYK equivalent. In fragrance packaging the usual casualties are saturated orange, deep purple and vivid turquoise. If one of those is your brand color, budget for a spot ink rather than being disappointed by a proof.

## Lamination

A thin plastic film bonded to the printed sheet. It protects the surface and it changes the color underneath it. All three remove the box from curbside recycling.

| Finish | Effect on color | Feel | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gloss | Deepens and saturates | Slick, hard | Throws highlights under studio lights |
| Matte | Lifts slightly, flattens contrast | Smooth, dry | Scuffs show on dark colors |
| Soft-touch | Mutes further than matte | Velvet, grippy | Marks with fingerprints, needs a keyline under foil |

The important point is that the same ink under three laminations gives three different colors. If your box is soft-touch and your bottle label is gloss, the identical brand color will read as two colors on the shelf.

## Surface effects

### Hot foil stamping

Metallic film pressed onto the board with a heated die. It is genuinely reflective in a way that metallic ink is not, which is why gold and silver effects are foiled rather than printed.

Foil needs an unlaminated keyline beneath it. Laid straight onto soft-touch film it lifts at the edges within weeks in warm storage, because the film's surface energy is too low to hold it. That keyline must exist in the artwork before we make plates.

### Spot UV

A clear gloss varnish applied to chosen areas only, almost always over a matte laminate. The effect is the contrast between the matte field and the glossy detail. On an already-glossy box it does nothing visible and is wasted money.

### Embossing and debossing

A die pressing the board up (emboss) or down (deboss) to create a physical impression. No ink is involved, so the effect is purely tactile and shadow-based.

Debossing reads unusually well in close-up video, where flat print tends to disappear. For brands whose packaging is filmed more often than it is held, it is often better value than another printed element.

### Die-cut window

An aperture cut into the panel to show the bottle. Left open it stays fully recyclable; covered with a clear film patch it protects against dust but adds a plastic component.

## Stacking finishes

Premium boxes routinely use several finishes together. A common stack is soft-touch as the base, spot UV on the wordmark, and foil on a rule or crest. Each is a separate pass with its own plate or die, and each adds cost and calendar time.

The order matters and so do the interactions. The most frequent failure is the foil-over-soft-touch problem above. The second most frequent is spot UV specified on a gloss box, where it is invisible. Both are caught at proof stage, but both are cheaper to avoid in the file.

## Proofing

A digital proof shows layout, text, position and structure. It does not show final color, because your monitor and our press are different devices with different capabilities.

For color-critical work, ask for a printed sample on your actual stock with your actual finish before the run. It adds a few days. Given that a color error is unrecoverable once thousands of boxes are printed, it is the cheapest insurance available on the job.
