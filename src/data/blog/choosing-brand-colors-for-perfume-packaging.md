# Choosing Brand Colors for Perfume Packaging

A color chosen on a screen is a set of light values. A color on a box is ink sitting on a surface. The two are related but they are not the same thing, and the gap between them is where most packaging disappointment lives.

## Why the screen lies

Your monitor emits light and mixes red, green and blue. A press lays ink and mixes cyan, magenta, yellow and black. RGB can produce colors that CMYK physically cannot reach.

In fragrance packaging the recurring casualties are:

- **Saturated orange** — comes back duller and browner than expected
- **Deep purple** — loses its intensity and drifts toward maroon
- **Vivid turquoise** — arguably the worst offender, rarely reproducible in process
- **Neon anything** — outside the range entirely

If one of these is your brand color, the answer is not a better proof. It is a Pantone spot ink, mixed to a published formula and printed from its own unit on the press.

## When to pay for a spot ink

Spot color costs more per plate. It is worth it when the color is doing brand work rather than decorative work.

The clearest test: does the same color appear anywhere else a customer will see it at the same time? A bottle label, a shipper, a shop fitting, last quarter's boxes. Process color drifts slightly between runs, which nobody notices in isolation and everybody notices side by side.

For a single decorative color in a multi-color design, process is fine and cheaper. For the color that *is* your brand, spot pays for itself the first time a reorder matches without a conversation.

## Colors that fight the press

**Deep blacks.** Sent as 100% K alone, black prints flat and slightly grey. A rich black adds cyan underneath for depth. Any box with large black areas needs this, which is how our [matte black perfume cartons](/product/black-perfume-boxes/) are set up.

**Large solid areas.** Any color across a full panel shows everything: roller marks, density drift across the sheet, a speck of dust on the plate. This is where print quality is visible and where cheap printing gives itself away.

**Pale tints.** A pale pink is a light screen of ink, and light screens drift visibly when press density moves even slightly. Pastels need tighter density tolerance than deep colors, not looser.

## Finish changes the color

This surprises people more than anything else on the list. The same ink under three laminations produces three different colors.

Gloss deepens and saturates, so a mid blue goes richer. Matte scatters light, lifting the color slightly while flattening contrast. Soft-touch does what matte does and mutes it further.

The practical consequence: if your box is soft-touch and your bottle label is gloss, one brand color will read as two on the shelf. Decide the finish before you sign off the color, not after. The [print and finish glossary](/resources/print-and-finish-glossary/) sets out what each finish does in more detail.

Foil changes the calculation again, because foil reflects rather than absorbs. A gold you print and a gold you foil are not comparable, and if you want real metallic you foil it.

## Designing a color family

Brands running several fragrances usually want one structure and one layout in different colors per scent. Done well it reads as a collection. Done carelessly it reads as inconsistency.

The rule that makes it work is holding **ink coverage** roughly constant across the family. A design that is 90% coverage in deep purple and 20% coverage in yellow will not look like siblings, however identical the layout is, because the visual weight is completely different.

That means adjusting the artwork per color rather than recoloring one file and moving on. It is more work up front and it is the difference between a range and a set of unrelated boxes.

## Get a printed sample

A digital proof shows layout, position and text. It does not show final color, because your monitor and our press are different devices.

For anything color-critical, ask for a printed sample on your actual stock with your actual finish. It adds a few days to the schedule.

Set against the cost of discovering a colorway error after several thousand boxes are printed — which is unrecoverable, since the boxes cannot be reprinted or resold — it is the cheapest insurance on the whole job. You can request one alongside your specification through the [box configurator](/design-your-box/).

## A short working order

Pick the finish, then the color, then check the color exists in CMYK, then decide spot or process on whether it is a brand asset, then proof it on the real stock. Doing it in that order removes almost every color problem before it costs anything.
