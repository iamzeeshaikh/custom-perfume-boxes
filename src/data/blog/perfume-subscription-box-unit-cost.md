# Where the Money Goes in a Perfume Subscription Box

Retail packaging is judged on how it looks. Subscription packaging is judged on what it costs, because the same box ships every month to every member and the arithmetic compounds in a way retail never does.

## The number that changes everything

On a retail carton, ten cents is a rounding error. On a subscription box at 3,000 members shipping monthly, ten cents is $3,600 a year.

That single fact should drive every decision in this category, and it explains why subscription packaging looks different from retail packaging even when the brand is identical.

## Savings that are real

**Structure.** Dropping from a rigid box to a strong folding mailer is the largest single saving available and it is usually invisible to the customer, because a subscription box is opened at home rather than compared on a shelf.

**Ink coverage.** Going from four-color to one or two colors removes plates and press passes. A two-color design on good board can look better than a four-color design on cheap board, so this is not automatically a downgrade.

**Lamination.** Dropping film removes a process. It also makes the box recyclable, which subscription audiences tend to care about more than retail ones.

**Size.** Courier pricing works in dimensional bands. A box half an inch smaller that lands in a cheaper band saves more per shipment than almost anything you can do to the box itself. Send us your carrier's bands with your product dimensions and we will size the carton to fit inside the cheaper one where the contents allow.

## Savings that cost more than they save

**Thinning the board.** This is the tempting one and it is almost always wrong. A crushed box costs a replacement product, a replacement box, return freight and a customer service conversation. One failure wipes out the saving on several hundred boxes.

**Dropping the insert.** A vial that moves in transit either breaks or arrives with a loosened atomizer, and the customer attributes that to the fragrance rather than to the packaging. You lose the product, the shipment and some of the relationship.

**Cheaper tape closures.** A closure that needs packing tape across the artwork undoes the design you paid for and reads as cheap at the exact moment the customer is forming an impression.

## The box travels alone

A retail carton moves on a pallet inside an outer case. A subscription box goes through automated sortation on its own, gets thrown onto a belt, sits under heavier parcels overnight and lands on a doorstep.

It is its own transit packaging. That means E-flute corrugated for anything containing glass, a closure that survives handling without tape, and an insert that immobilizes rather than merely presents. ## Refreshing the design without reprinting everything

Most subscription brands cannot justify new plates every month. The model that works is a fixed printed outer ordered in bulk, plus a cheap variable element inside.

That variable element can be a digitally printed card, a sticker, a belly band or a printed sleeve. Digital printing has no plate cost, so short runs of the variable piece are genuinely cheap.

The result is monthly freshness at a fraction of the cost, and it lets you order the outer in a quantity that reaches a real price break. Six months of outers and twelve weeks of cards is a common and sensible split.

## The opening gets filmed

Subscription boxes are opened on camera more than any other packaging format, which makes the opening sequence a design decision rather than an accident.

What works: a clean tear strip or a lid that lifts without a struggle, something printed on the inside of the lid, and products held in position rather than loose. What fails: tape across the print, a box that needs scissors, and vials rolling out when the lid comes off.

Interior print is cheap and it is the part that gets photographed. If you are deciding where a small budget goes, it goes there rather than on a second exterior finish. The same logic drives how we build [discovery set presentation boxes](/product/perfume-discovery-boxes/).

## Working out your own numbers

Take your subscriber count, multiply by twelve, and multiply that by each decision you are considering. A choice that saves eight cents looks trivial until you see the annual figure, and a choice that risks a two percent damage rate looks harmless until you cost the replacements.

Both calculations take about a minute and they usually settle the specification faster than any amount of discussion about how the box looks.
